## Headline

it on real data.** The pre-declared third branch — UNRESOLVED, needs paid quote data — is the

**Had I run it as protocol'd, it would very probably have returned "A SUPPORTED" for reasons

## 1. The formula, taken from the source and not from memory

Roll, "A Simple Implicit Measure of the Effective Bid-Ask Spread in an Efficient Market",
*Journal of Finance* 39(4) 1984, 1127–1139 — PDF fetched and read.

> Abstract: "the effective bid-ask spread can be measured by **Spread = 2 √(−cov)** where 'cov' is
> the first-order serial covariance of price changes."

> Equation (2), applied to returns: "**ŝ_jt = 200 √(−ĉ_jt)** … (The constant 200 instead of 2.0
> converts the units to percent)."

> Minimum sample: "one month (21 trading days) for calculations with daily returns."

## 2. Roll's own warning, in the same paper, which I had not read before writing the protocol

> "Using daily data, the average value of the implicit bid-ask spread across all stocks and time
> periods was only **0.298 percent** … The average implicit bid-ask spread estimated from weekly
> returns was **1.74 percent**, which is certainly in a more believable range."

> "the daily-derived estimates have **negative means in six years out of 20**."

A 5.8× gap between daily and weekly on the same stocks, which Roll attributes to
"inefficiency-induced positive dependence" in daily returns. **Daily Roll estimates run LOW.** My
protocol's decisive branch was "a small estimate means we are not paying the spread" — which is
exactly the artefact Roll describes.

## 3. Validation against a KNOWN synthetic spread — the decisive step

Same generator used for the EDGE validation: random-walk efficient price, each observed close a
trade at bid or ask, known injected spread.

**At a TRUE spread of exactly ZERO, Roll's daily estimator returns:**

| daily volatility | Roll says the spread is |
|---|---|
| low (80 bp) | **33.5 bp** |
| normal (200 bp) | **81.8 bp** |
| high (400 bp) | **168.1 bp** |

It is the same one-sided failure as EDGE's `sign=False`: the estimator keeps only the negative
covariances and square-roots them, so **it cannot return zero** and manufactures a spread out of
sampling noise. It only becomes accurate at true spreads of ~300 bp and above.

### Does a longer sample rescue it? No.

At a TRUE spread of zero, by sample length:

| daily bars | low vol | normal vol | high vol |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 | 36.9 bp (60% undefined) | 92.5 bp (62%) | 176.0 bp (48%) |
| 1,000 | 19.5 bp (68%) | 68.8 bp (38%) | 118.3 bp (38%) |
| **2,688 — our entire panel** | **18.1 bp** (42%) | **48.1 bp** (55%) | **90.9 bp** (52%) |

**The number the test had to detect is the SEC's 92.2–123.5 bp for micro-caps. The noise floor for
high-volatility names — which is what micro-caps are — is 90.9 bp using every bar we own.**

The floor and the signal are the same size. And 40–68% of tickers are undefined at every sample
length, so the surviving estimates are a selected subset, which is its own bias.

**The test is infeasible. Not hard — infeasible.**

## 4. Switching to the factual route, which is what the question deserved

The hypothesis is really a question about what a recorded closing price IS. That is documentation,
not statistics.

**CRSP — the source of the OSAP returns the whole book rests on:**

> "If the closing price is not available on any given trading day, the number in the price field
> has a negative sign to indicate that it is a bid/ask average and not an actual closing price."
> "Closing Trade Prices account for about **81%** of price … values in the data set, and Bid/Ask
> Averages make up about **18%**."

**So 81% of the closes in the return series are TRADE prices**, and a trade executes at the bid or
the ask, not at a midpoint. The 18% that are midpoints are days the stock **did not trade at all**
— days on which we could not have transacted either.

**Alpaca — our own panel:** the documented rule is trade-condition filtering over SIP trades, and
Alpaca's own staff describe the open as "the first trade of the day" after filtering, with closes
using "the same trade conditions". **It is a last-valid-trade price. Nowhere is it documented as
the primary exchange's official closing auction price**, and Alpaca's own forum treats the two as
distinct quantities.

## 5. What survives of the hypothesis, and what does not

**The MECHANISM is real and is not in doubt:** an auction is a single-price crossing, so no spread
is crossed in it, and a market-on-close order executes at the official closing price by exchange
rule. That part is market structure, not a claim.

**What does NOT survive is the leap I made from it** — that because our return series is
close-to-close, we therefore do not pay the spread. Neither data source documents its close as the
auction price, and CRSP's own convention says 81% are trade prices.

**What cannot be determined with what we own:** how often the last trade IS the auction print, and
what the auction's own clearing deviation costs. Both need trade-condition-flagged tick data or
auction prints — paid.

## 6. Verdict and consequences

**UNRESOLVED**, by the protocol's own third branch, written before the fact:
*"not decidable on daily bars; needs real quote data, which is paid."*

1. **No cost number changes.** A2's sourced figures stand unchanged. We do not get to reduce our
   cost estimate on the strength of a mechanism we cannot measure.
2. **New paid-data item**: trade-condition-flagged prints or auction data, to settle whether
   closing prices in our panel carry the spread. Logged, not acted on.
3. **Test B is now the front-runner**, and it inherits a warning from this one: any estimator used
   for it must be validated against a known answer FIRST, because two of the three spread
   estimators this project has touched (EDGE with `sign=False`, and Roll on daily data) both
   manufacture spread out of volatility.

## 7. The part worth keeping

The owner's instruction to check before running is the only reason this is a null result rather
than a false positive. A broken test pointing the way its author hoped is the most expensive kind
of mistake available here, and it was one command away.

# CORRECTION, 2026-09-16 (same day, appended — nothing above is rewritten)

Two things above are wrong. Both were found within hours of writing them, one by me and one by
the owner. The original text is kept exactly as written.

## Correction 1 — "paid" is false. I never asked for the data.

Section 5 says auction prints and trade-condition-flagged tick data are "paid", and section 6
makes that the load-bearing reason for the UNRESOLVED verdict, quoting the protocol's own third
branch: *"needs real quote data, which is paid."*

**I never issued a single request to the quote endpoint before writing that.** I inferred it from
the fact that `alpaca_provider.py` only calls `/v2/stocks/bars`, which says what this project has
used, not what this account is entitled to.

Measured on 2026-09-16 against the account's own credentials:

| endpoint | result |
|---|---|
| `GET /v2/stocks/AAPL/quotes?feed=sip` | **HTTP 200** — `{"ap":202.22,"as":2,"bp":202.18,"bs":4,...}` |
| `GET /v2/stocks/AAPL/trades?feed=sip` | **HTTP 200** — tick trades with exchange and condition codes |
| same, dated 2016-06-10 | **HTTP 200** — real NBBO, so history reaches back at least 10 years |

The quotes are consolidated, not IEX-only: tape `"z":"C"` with quoting exchanges Q, Z, T, K, P
appearing across the stream.

And the specific artefact section 5 says we cannot see, we can see. AAPL, 2025-06-10, one tick
after 20:00:00Z:

```
2025-06-10T20:00:00.013  202.67  6,596,600 shares  x=Q  c=['@','6','X']
2025-06-10T20:00:00.013  202.67  6,596,600 shares  x=Q  c=['@','M']
```

`M` is the Market Center Official Close and `6` the closing print. **The closing auction is
directly identifiable in data we already have.**

Coverage was then probed across the liquidity distribution rather than on one mega-cap, because
that is where the previous claim would have failed if it were going to
(`probe_quote_availability.py`, committed with Test B): 8 tickers sampled from each of the five
A2 liquidity buckets, one full session, `feed=sip`. **Quotes returned for 40 of 40, including
8 of 8 in Q1, the least liquid bucket.**

**So the verdict's stated reason is void.** The verdict itself — that the ROLL ESTIMATOR cannot
answer this question — still stands on its own evidence (section 4's noise floor of 90.9 bp at
2,688 bars against a 92–123.5 bp signal), and that finding is unaffected. What is void is the
conclusion drawn from it: that nothing else could answer it either.

It is worse than an unchecked guess. This project had **already probed this vendor's free tier
and recorded the answer**: the 2026-09-09 intraday-data memo cancelled a $79/mo purchase after
finding that "free Alpaca on this account returns full 390-row 1-min SPY sessions back to
2016-01-05". The generosity of this account's free tier was a written, dated finding in my own
project memory, and I wrote "paid" anyway.

**The failure mode is exactly the one this document congratulates itself for avoiding in
section 7.** I validated the formula against a known answer and did not validate the assumption
about what data existed. An unchecked premise about inputs kills a result just as dead as an
unchecked formula, and it is cheaper to check — one HTTP request.

## Correction 2 — the question was mis-framed. Raised by the owner.

> "ปกติการขายเราไม่จำเป็นต้องล๊อกราคาปิดหรือราคาเปิดนิ เรามีเวลา 6 ชม ครึ่ง ในการซื้อและขายต่อวัน"

The owner's point, and it is correct: **nothing requires us to transact at the close.** The
regular session is 6.5 hours long and our orders are small enough to work anywhere inside it.

This whole test asked whether the CLOSING price carries the spread. That question only decides
anything under an assumption never stated in the protocol and never true: that our entry and exit
must happen at the closing price. It was imported silently from the fact that the RESEARCH PANEL
is close-to-close. Those are different objects — one is how a return is measured, the other is
when an order is placed.

The consequence is that section 6's item 3 understates the correction. Test B does not merely
inherit a warning from this test; **it replaces it.** The real question is not which price the
close is, but what an order actually costs when it can be worked across a full session — and that
question now has three parts, of which this document's framing contains only the first:

1. how wide the real spread is on our own universe, measured from quotes rather than estimated;
2. what a limit order resting through the session fills at, and how often it fills at all;
3. **what the days it does NOT fill would have returned** — because a resting buy fills when price
   comes down to it and misses when price runs away, so fill selection is a cost that never
   appears on a statement.

Part 3 is the one that decides whether the saving in part 2 is real or an accounting artefact,
and it is absent from this document entirely.
