SOURCE PAPER, READ DIRECTLY, NOT SUMMARIZED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Field, Laura Casares; Hanka, Gordon. "The Expiration of IPO Share Lockups."
  Journal of Finance 56(2), April 2001, pp. 471-500. Full PDF read directly,
  all 30 pages (pages 1-2 read before this run per the commissioning brief;
  pages 3-16 and 25-30 read in this run, covering every table, the
  methodology section, the hypothesis tests, the conclusion, and the
  appendix). Headline numbers already quoted correctly in the commissioning
  brief; nothing found in this run's own reading contradicts them. Two
  results not in the brief, both load-bearing for a future build:
    Table I (p.476): composition of lockup length by issue year -- 43% at
      180 days in 1988, rising to 91% by 1996, 80% by 1997 (their sample's
      last full year). "<180 / 180 / >180" shares for the full 1988-1997
      sample: 11% / 80% / 9%.
    Table III (p.481): the -1/+1 CAR split by lockup length -- 180-day
      lockups show a LARGER effect (-1.3%, t=-3.1, n=214 for <180-day) than
      >180-day lockups (-0.8%, t=-1.6, n=173) among the exactly-fitting
      subsample the authors report. The paper's own robustness section D.1
      (p.484) directly tests and rejects a generic "day-180 effect" (firms
      IPO'd without any lockup show an insignificant -0.4% at day
      175-181), so the effect is attributed to the lockup mechanism
      specifically, not a calendar artifact.
    Section VI Conclusion (p.495-496), quoted directly: the authors state
      plainly they find "several hypotheses receiving limited support" but
      "no complete explanation" -- downward-sloping demand curves get some
      support (larger negative CAR on higher-volume days) but cannot fully
      account for it (a significant negative CAR persists even when 3-day
      volume is under 1% of float); worse-than-expected insider-sale news
      gets some support too but the effect survives even with zero
      disclosed insider sales. This is the original authors' own honest
      admission of an unresolved mechanism -- carried forward here per this
      project's zero-fabrication rule, not glossed over.

  PREVIOUSLY-UNDOCUMENTED risk sits alongside it -- TICKER RECYCLING AND
  RENAMING actively produces silently WRONG classifications, not merely
  missing ones. Specifics:

    * An UNBIASED, systematically-sampled set of 37 ordinary (non-SPAC,
      non-cherry-picked) IPOs from 2012-2019 hit at 40.5% -- the single most
      decision-relevant number in this report, since it is not selected
      toward either survivors or failures.
    * Split empirically by whether a ticker resolves to ANY data in the last
      30 days ("still trading today" proxy): 64.3% lockup-window hit rate
      for the "still trading" bucket vs. 10.7% for the "not" bucket -- a
      ~6x gap, the direct, measured signature of survivorship bias.
    * NEW FINDING, not previously logged for any family in this project:
      Facebook's OWN 2012 IPO ticker "FB" was independently confirmed, live,
      to now resolve to an entirely unrelated ETF (ProShares S&P 500 Dynamic
      Daily Buffer ETF) after Meta's 2022 rename freed the symbol; Pandora
      Media's "P" now resolves to an unrelated company (Everpure, Inc.).
      Both were confirmed by directly querying yfinance's own `.info` for
      long name and quote type -- not inferred. This means a naive "is this
      ticker still resolvable today" check is not just imperfect for
      delisted names (the already-logged P2 finding) -- for a RENAMED or
      RECYCLED ticker it actively returns a false "yes, still trading",
      attributing a THIRD PARTY'S current existence to the original IPO
      company. This is worse than missingness because it is silent and
      would not raise any error a pipeline could catch.
    * A qualifying nuance, also measured, not assumed: NOT every
      delisted/acquired company loses its historical archive. Two names in
      the systematic sample -- Foundation Medicine (acquired by Roche,
      2018) and Hortonworks (merged into Cloudera, 2019) -- correctly
      resolved real, plausible historical prices for their own 2013-2014
      lockup windows while independently confirmed (via yfinance's own
      metadata) to be non-live, retired tickers today. Coverage of delisted
      names is real but INCONSISTENT and unpredictable from outside this
      project's control, not a clean "always missing" rule -- which is
      itself an argument for querying the target historical window directly
      rather than inferring availability from whether a ticker is "alive
      today" (this run's own Section C design does the former; a build
      using the latter shortcut would get exactly the FB/P-style false
      positives above).

  Given all of this: the family is BUILDABLE on free data, would carry a
  real, honestly non-trivial sample-size loss and a directionally
  UNRESOLVED (see Q3 discussion below) survivorship bias, and needs a
  construction discipline this run did not have to invent (CIK/name-based
  identity verification, not a raw ticker-string join) that a future build
  task must implement, not assume away.

Q2. IS "IPO DATE + 180 CALENDAR DAYS" A DEFENSIBLE LOCKUP PROXY?
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  YES, PROVISIONALLY, on the source paper's own Table I evidence (quoted
  above): 80-91% of lockups in the paper's later years (1996-1997) run
  exactly 180 days, and the -1/+1 CAR is directionally negative and
  significant in the <180, =180 (implied), and >180 buckets alike (Table
  III), so the approximation does not manufacture the effect from nothing.

  This run did NOT verify that the ~90% 180-day share still holds in years
  after Field & Hanka's 1997 sample ends (i.e., in the 2012-2019+ window
  any free-data build would actually use) -- that is an open question, not
  an assumption carried over uninspected.

  SEC EDGAR full-text search CAN answer this for free, confirmed live in
  this run, with two real limits:
    * SEC's own documentation (sec.gov/edgar/search, cross-checked via
      WebSearch 2026-09-06) states full-text search covers filings
      SUBMITTED SINCE 2001-05-04 -- confirmed empirically here too (a query
      for "lock-up" in 424B4 filings restricted to 2001 alone returned 136
      hits; the underlying paper's own 1988-1997 sample predates this
      coverage entirely, so EDGAR full-text search could never validate
      Field & Hanka's OWN sample, only a modern one).
    * The exact phrase '"lock-up period of 180 days"' in 424B4 filings,
      ALL YEARS 2001-present, returned only 47 hits -- LOW relative to the
      roughly 150-200/year non-SPAC IPO count this run separately measures
      (Q4), meaning prospectus boilerplate phrases the lockup length many
      different ways ("180 days after the date of this prospectus", "the
      180th day following...", etc.). Exact-phrase search UNDER-COUNTS
      badly; a real bulk-extraction pipeline would need a broader regex/NLP
      pass across many phrasings, not one exact string. That confirms the
      brief's own expectation that bulk extraction carries real effort, and
      is a reason to prefer the free, literature-backed 180-day
      approximation over building an extraction pipeline, at least for a
      first build -- not a reason the exact-length path is unreachable.

  declines to guess. Field & Hanka's own Table III (p.481) shows firms
  whose price had RISEN since IPO carry a LARGER negative CAR (-1.7%) than

  characterizes the specific firms this project's free data loses (that

  the real build must treat the sign and magnitude of this bias as

  Combining the 2012-2019 non-SPAC count (856 + 627 = 1,483 over 8 years,
  ~185/year) with this run's measured 40.5% systematic hit rate gives an
  order-of-magnitude estimate of roughly 600 USABLE lockup-expiration events
  over that 8-year window, or ~75/year -- comparable in SHAPE to Field &
  Hanka's own ~195/year average over 1988-1997 (their number is larger
  because CRSP, their paid data source, does not lose delisted names the
  way this project's free path does). ~75 independent, one-shot
  per-company events per year is a real but noticeably thinner annual
  sample than this project's typical S&P-500-cross-sectional families (where
  every constituent contributes an observation every period); a future
  build's DSR/effective-N reasoning should treat this as ~600 one-shot
  events pooled across 8 years, not as repeated monthly cross-sections, and
  should not borrow another family's effective-N intuition uninspected.

WHAT A BUILD TASK WOULD NEED TO KNOW, IF THIS PROCEEDS -- SKETCHED, NOT BUILT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Universe construction: Ritter's IPO-age.xlsx, filtered to non-SPAC
     (needs a better filter than this run's crude heuristic -- possibly
     cross-referencing SEC's company_tickers.json / EDGAR filing type, since
     a SPAC's registration statement is typically an S-1 for a blank-check
     shell with characteristic language, not a reliable name/ticker
     pattern), restricted to a year window with adequate free-data-era
     price coverage (this run used 2012-2019; whether to extend earlier or
     later is a build-time decision, not resolved here).
  2. Identity resolution MUST be CIK- or exact-name-based, not a raw ticker
     join -- per this run's own FB/P/SNOW/GPRO/PTON findings. Concretely:
     resolve each Ritter row to a CIK via SEC's company_tickers.json (by
     name match at IPO time, imperfect but far better than none) or via
     Ritter's own CRSP PermNo column (present in the file but only useful
     if this project ever gains CRSP-linked price access -- it does not
     today). Any ticker-string hit should be treated as PROVISIONAL until
     the resolved security's name/quoteType is spot-checked against the
     Ritter row's company name, exactly as this run did for FB/P/FMI/HDP.
  3. Lockup date: IPO date + 180 calendar days as the default, with the
     option (not required for a first build) to refine via SEC EDGAR
     full-text search on the company's own 424B4/424B1 for an exact length,
     acknowledging the phrasing-variability problem measured in Q2.
  4. Event window: Field & Hanka use day -1 to +1 (their headline -1.5%/
     -1.9% numbers) and day -5 to +1 as a robustness window; both are
     directly reproducible from a daily close series, no intraday data
     needed.
  5. Benchmark: Field & Hanka use the CRSP value-weighted index; this
     project's nearest free equivalent would be a broad-market ETF or index
     proxy already wired into its market-data infrastructure -- not
     selected here, a build-time decision.
  6. Cross-sectional splits worth testing given the data actually
     available: VC-backed vs not (the paper's largest documented effect,
     and Ritter's file flags it directly), and the 4/2012-2019 windows
     against the earlier lockup-length composition if EDGAR-derived exact
     lengths are ever built.
  7. Mechanism characterization for CLAUDE.md Section 4's mechanism-fidelity
     review: this is NOT a forced/mechanical sale the way N-PORT
     flow-induced trading or margin-liquidation candidates are -- insiders
     CHOOSE whether to sell once the lockup permits it, and the paper's own
     authors state they have no complete causal explanation (Section VI).
     A build should characterize this honestly as "pre-scheduled,
     publicly-disclosed supply-shock risk with a documented but incompletely
     understood price effect," not force it into either the "informed
     positioning" or "purely mechanical flow" box this session's other
     candidates have used.
  8. THE SURVIVORSHIP-BIAS AND TICKER-IDENTITY CAVEATS ABOVE MUST BE CARRIED
     INTO ANY DSR/preservation_score verdict on this family as a named,
     first-class limitation -- not a footnote -- given how large the
     measured effect is (37.5-40.5% overall/unbiased hit rate; 0% on every
     verified-delisted anchor).

WHAT THIS RUN DOES NOT ESTABLISH
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  It does not compute an abnormal return, a CAR, or any DSR/
  preservation_score number for any company. It does not build or backtest
  a lockup-expiration signal. It does not resolve the exact 180-day-share
  question for the 2012-2019+ era (only confirms EDGAR full-text search
  could answer it, with real phrasing-variability caveats). It does not
  fully re-verify company identity for all 56 sample rows -- only FB, P,
  FMI, and HDP were individually cross-checked via `.info`; the remaining
  hits are presumed correct on the strength of Ritter's own data quality
  (spot-checked separately, Q1) but a production build must verify more
  broadly, not fewer than this run did. It does not determine the
  DIRECTION of the survivorship bias's effect on any future result -- that
  is named explicitly as unknown, not assumed conservative. Its SPAC filter
  is a heuristic (name/ticker pattern), not a first-party classification,
  and a real build needs a better one. Its account of SEC EDGAR full-text
  search coverage is bounded by what two live queries plus SEC's own
  published documentation could establish in this run; a full historical
  audit of phrasing variants was not attempted.
