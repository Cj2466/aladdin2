## The cross-section

One-way HALF-spread in basis points, by quintile of median daily dollar volume:

| bucket | n | median $ volume | median price | tick floor | EDGE (truncated) | cells with a positive estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 least liquid | 871 | 108,144 | 2.72 | **18.38** | **106.02** | 80.7% |
| Q2 | 871 | 1,325,642 | 6.73 | 7.43 | 37.21 | 68.2% |
| Q3 | 871 | 9,357,120 | 17.21 | 2.90 | 21.54 | 61.6% |
| Q4 | 871 | 44,000,129 | 40.25 | 1.24 | 21.12 | 62.9% |
| Q5 most liquid | 871 | 241,193,116 | 101.85 | **0.49** | **16.25** | 59.7% |

### Read this table with the estimator's known limits, not as five equal facts

The tick floor spans **37×** across the buckets (18.38 → 0.49); EDGE spans only
**6.5×** (106 → 16). That compression is the volatility noise floor the synthetic
validation measured: at a TRUE spread of zero, EDGE returns a positive number
proportional to the stock's volatility. So:

- **Q1–Q2: EDGE is informative.** A 100 bp half-spread on a $2.72 stock trading
  $108k a day is economically plausible and far above the tick floor.
- **Q4–Q5: EDGE is its own noise floor.** 16 bp on a $102 stock trading $241m a
  day is not credible; published sources put the S&P 500 one-way half-spread at
  **1.3–2.3 bp** (Hagstromer JFE 2021 Table 1; Mackintosh/Nasdaq 2024). EDGE
  overstates there by roughly an order of magnitude.

Confirmed by regression on 4,330 tickers: log(EDGE) is 56% explained by
volatility alone (slope +0.85 on log vol, i.e. near-proportional — the floor's
signature), while dollar volume adds an incremental R² of **0.314** after
volatility is removed. EDGE carries real liquidity information; it is just
buried under a volatility-proportional floor that dominates for liquid names.

## The finding that actually decides what to build

The cost uncertainty divides by the rebalance period. At the measured bracket:

| rebalance | assumed 20/P | measured LOW /P | measured HIGH /P | Sharpe @LOW | Sharpe @HIGH |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| monthly (P=1) | 20.0 | 11.1 | 112.4 | 0.727 | **−0.719** |
| quarterly (P=3) | 6.7 | 3.7 | 37.5 | 0.832 | 0.350 |
| annual (P=12) | 1.7 | **0.9** | **9.4** | 0.871 | **0.751** |
| 3-yearly (P=36) | 0.6 | 0.3 | 3.1 | 0.880 | 0.840 |

(Illustrative, holding every member at one cadence; the exact re-pricing above
uses each predictor's own `Portfolio Period`.)

**An annually-rebalanced book passes at BOTH ends of the measured bracket. A
monthly book's verdict is entirely decided by where in the bracket the truth
lies — and at the high end it loses money.**

This converges independently with the 2026-09-13 holding-period result, which
reached the same conclusion from the edge side (gross edge per trade rises 8.4×
from monthly to annual while gross Sharpe stays flat). Two different
measurements, same instruction: **build slow members.**

## What A2 did NOT deliver, stated plainly

1. **No sourced level for anything but large caps.** The bracket is wide because
   its upper end is an estimator artefact, not a measurement. Closing it needs a
   published spread statistic by size/liquidity decile — the single highest-value
   open item, and a literature task, not a compute task.
2. **No intraday data.** A daily-bar estimator cannot do better than this. The
   real fix is quote data, which is paid (logged, not acted on).
3. **Nothing about market impact.** Irrelevant at this account size, but the
   number is a spread cost only.
4. **Nothing about borrow.** The book is long-short; retail micro-cap borrow is
   frequently unavailable at any price. Unchanged from the audit's D6/P6.

# ADDENDUM (same day, 2026-09-15) — a SOURCED anchor was found, and it resolves the bracket downward

The body above closed by naming the highest-value open item: "a published spread
statistic by size/liquidity decile — a literature task, not a compute task."
That task was then done, and it changes the verdict.

## The source

**Collver, Charles, "A characterization of market quality for small capitalization
US equities", US SEC, Division of Trading and Markets, September 2014**
(`sec.gov/marketstructure/research/small_cap_liquidity.pdf`, fetched and read in
full). This is not an estimator: spreads are computed from the SEC's **own MIDAS
intraday trade-and-quote data** for all of 2013. Effective spread is defined as
2 × |trade price − prevailing midpoint|, dollar-value weighted.

The paper's own text, discussing Table 4:

> "Median relative effective spreads range from **0.922% to 1.235% for the
> smallest stocks** and from **0.045% to 0.210% for stocks in the $2 to $5
> Billion** capitalization range."

"Smallest" = market capitalization below $100 Million; the ranges span its price bins.
A relative effective spread is a FULL spread, so one-way half-spreads are half of these.

## Re-priced on the raw sealed series

| universe | one-way half-spread | one full reformation | book Sharpe | vs the 0.50 line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **cap < $100M** (SEC 2013 median) | **46.1 – 61.8 bp** | **184 – 247 bp** | **−1.53 to −0.95** | **FAIL, heavily** |
| cap $2–5B (SEC 2013 median) | 2.2 – 10.5 bp | 9 – 42 bp | 0.39 – 0.70 | borderline |
| S&P 500 (Hagstromer JFE 2021 / Mackintosh 2024) | 1.3 – 2.3 bp | 5 – 9 bp | 0.70 – 0.74 | **PASS** |

Break-even remains 30.6 bp per reformation.

### Where my EDGE bracket landed against the source

For Q1 (median price $2.72, median $108k daily volume — the micro-cap corner),
my measured bracket was tick floor **18.4** → EDGE **106** bp half-spread. The
SEC's sourced median for sub-$100M caps is **46–62 bp**. **The sourced number
lands inside my bracket**, about 2× above my exact lower bound and about half my
estimator's upper bound. So the estimator was the right order of magnitude where
it is informative, and the bracket was honest — just wide.

## What this means, stated without softening

**OSAP builds 210 of its 242 predictors EQUAL-WEIGHTED on CRSP.** The typical
member *by count* in an equal-weighted CRSP cross-section is a micro-cap. That is
the first row of the table — **the book as measured loses money at retail cost,
by a wide margin, not by a hair.**

The 0.599 result was never wrong arithmetic; the audit reproduced it exactly. It
was answering a question with an unsourced cost input, and the sourced input is
5–12× larger for the universe the book is actually built on.

### The caveat that stops this from becoming the opposite error

The S&P 500 row PASSES at 0.70–0.74 — better than the original 0.599 — and it is
tempting to conclude "just build it on liquid names."

**That row is not a valid estimate of a large-cap book.** It charges large-cap
COSTS against gross returns generated by a micro-cap-weighted portfolio. The
size interaction runs the wrong way for us: published anomalies are generally
stronger in small caps, which is much of why OSAP equal-weights. A liquid-only
book would pay the cheap row's costs AND earn a smaller, unmeasured gross edge.

OSAP publishes one series per predictor, not one per size bucket, so **the gross
edge of these anomalies restricted to liquid names cannot be recovered from this
dataset at all.** It has to be rebuilt on our own panel — which is what the
member factory was always going to do, now with a specific question to answer
first.
